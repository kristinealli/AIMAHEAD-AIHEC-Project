Unraveling
the Patterns.

Predicting frequent mental distress across survey years and assessing model reliability for American Indian and Alaska Native adults.

Machine learning can help identify patterns in health data that can be useful for understanding community needs, planning services, and directing resources.

American Indian and Alaska Native (AI/AN) adults experience differences in mental-health outcomes and access to care, highlighting the need for research that examines population-specific health patterns.

The question beneath the numbers

Population patterns aren't always population-neutral.

Research using 2018–2020 BRFSS data found frequent mental distress among 19.1% of AI/AN adults versus 13.3% of U.S. adults overall-a 5.8 percentage-point difference. [3] When a model learns from a national dataset, we need to examine its performance for populations individually, not only in aggregate.

03 / Unsupervised exploration

Looking for structure
before the labels.

Before modeling FMD directly, the project explored whether health and social characteristics naturally grouped AI/AN respondents into meaningful profiles. Mental-health measures were deliberately excluded from clustering and examined afterwards.

2023–2025 AI/AN exploration: 25,080 respondents across those survey years (2023: 8,951; 2024: 9,156; 2025: 6,973). Variables covered physical health, chronic conditions, functional health, socioeconomic factors, healthcare access, health activity, and demographics.

Method: variables were encoded, scaled, and domain-balanced; principal component analysis (PCA) reduced dimensionality, and k-means identified two profiles using the 2023 data.

✳

What PCA told us: The first principal component was dominated by physically unhealthy days, with general health, exercise, and difficulty walking also contributing. The second reflected primarily exercise behavior. These are associations-not evidence that physical health causes mental distress.

06 / What the data cannot say

Study Decisions &
Implications

Data availability, sample definition, and self-reporting all shape the patterns a model can learn. Careful interpretation of the results and knowing what and what they do not imply is important.

01

Different people each year

BRFSS is repeated cross-sectional data. The model classifies self-reported FMD in survey records from different adults each year, not longitudinally following the same individuals over time. We are therefore observing population-level patterns, not individual trajectories.

02

Not every identity is represented

The main comparison excludes multiracial and unresolved classifications because AI/AN combinations included in the "Mixed Race" category were not consistently identifiable across BRFSS survey years. This narrows the population represented.

03

Not all experiences are measured

Historical trauma, discrimination, community connection, and cultural continuity can shape health, but they are not adequately represented by the six supervised predictors.

04

Survey years are not identical

Question wording, coding, sampling, response patterns, and available jurisdictions change over time. Variables were harmonized where possible, but the comparisons may still retain limitations.

Data completeness

Missing doesn't mean
meaningless.

In 2025, household income had approximately 19% missingness in both populations. Physically unhealthy days were missing more often among AI/AN respondents (approximately 3.4%) than among other single-race respondents (approximately 2.3%).

For supervised modeling, missing predictor categories were retained (considered as information) rather than silently discarded. The quality and availability of measurements still affects interpretation.

✳
An important perspective

Data should serve
the communities it represents.

These analyses use publicly available national survey data, not data governed by a Tribal Nation. The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance offer a framework grounded in Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, and Ethics. [5]

National models can help identify important questions, but they should never replace Tribal knowledge, sovereignty, or community-defined priorities.

While populations may share many of the same predictors of mental distress, shared risk factors do not necessarily translate to equal prevalence. Some communities may experience these factors more frequently or face additional circumstances that national models fail to capture.

This project was developed as an educational research exercise through the AIM-AHEAD AIHEC initiative to build practical skills in data science, statistical analysis, and machine learning. It uses publicly available data from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) to explore population-level patterns and demonstrate both the potential and limitations of national health data.

07 / The takeaways

What did
we unravel?

01

Prediction improved.

The selected model reduced national Brier error by 16.2% relative to a historical-prevalence-only benchmark (approximate paired 95% CI: 15.5%–16.8%).

02

Reliability needs a closer look.

AI/AN AUROC was lower in the observed results, but its difference from the comparison group was not statistically established at the reported confidence level.

03

Context matters as much as code.

Physical health emerged as an important predictor. But predictors alone cannot explain structural or community differences, and national datasets do not capture every lived experience.
